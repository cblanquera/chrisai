# Article Writing

Create an article that preserves the author's technical depth while giving a
broader reader a clear path into the idea.

## 1. Establish The Article

Identify from existing context:

- the central claim, lesson, or question
- the intended reader and what they probably know already
- what the reader should understand, feel, or be able to explain afterward
- the evidence available: examples, experience, data, code, sources, or
  concrete outcomes
- the intended destination, if formatting depends on it

Ask only for a missing input that would substantially change the argument.

## 2. Find The Human Entry Point

Start from something concrete: a surprising outcome, recognizable problem,
specific observation, failed assumption, useful artifact, or tension between
what people expect and what actually happens.

Do not begin with industry background, generic importance, company history, or
an announcement formula. Explain context after the reader has a reason to
care.

## 3. Build The Argument

Create an outline with one job per section. Follow the reader's likely
questions rather than the author's internal project chronology.

Useful questions include:

1. What is happening, and why should I care?
2. How does it work in terms I can picture?
3. What makes this difficult or counterintuitive?
4. What alternatives or trade-offs matter?
5. What changed in the author's thinking?
6. What can the reader do or notice next?

Use only the questions that serve the piece.

## 4. Translate Without Flattening

Apply `../references/accessible-technical-voice.md` to replace unexplained
jargon with context, analogy, consequences, or a progressive explanation.
Keep precise terms when they are necessary, then make them understandable.

Use examples before abstractions where possible. Let the author's reasoning,
uncertainty, and opinion appear throughout the article rather than only in the
introduction and conclusion.

## 5. Draft For Online Reading

- Give the title a specific claim, tension, story, or payoff.
- Make headings communicate an idea rather than label a generic section.
- Keep paragraphs focused and break at meaningful changes in direction.
- Prefer evidence and observable consequences over promotional adjectives.
- Explain limitations and rejected alternatives when they strengthen trust.
- Use code, screenshots, diagrams, or data only when they help the reader
  understand the argument.

## 6. Close The Conversation

End with a useful consequence, next step, unresolved tension, or genuine
invitation to compare experiences. A question is welcome when the author truly
wants the answer and it follows naturally from the article.

Do not append a generic recap, motivational flourish, product pitch, or
engagement prompt unrelated to the argument.

## 7. Quality Gate

Before delivering, verify that:

- the article makes one coherent argument
- the opening earns attention without hype
- a non-specialist can follow the mechanism and stakes
- an expert would not feel the idea was made false by simplification
- factual claims are supported or clearly marked as interpretation
- every section contributes something new
- the author's voice and point of view persist throughout
- the ending feels conversational rather than transactional
